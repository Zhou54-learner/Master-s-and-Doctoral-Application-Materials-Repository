## Statement

When I began my undergraduate thesis, I treated electromagnetic simulation mainly as a tool for evaluating candidate absorber structures. I worked with an existing Python–CST workflow, extended the design variables and geometric constraints, and automated parts of the simulation and data-processing process. Most of the time, the practical goal was straightforward: make the workflow run reliably and use it to search for better designs.

One part of the project changed how I thought about simulation. I compared thin-film and Ohmic Sheet representations of the same resistive structure and found that the modelling choice could change the predicted reflection response and even which designs appeared preferable in the optimization. That made me realize that a numerical result is not separate from the assumptions and numerical treatment behind it. Since then, I have become increasingly interested in what lies between a physical model and the final curve returned by a solver.

For doctoral study, I would like to understand that layer more deeply in frequency-domain wave problems. Rather than treating Helmholtz and Maxwell solvers as black boxes, I want to learn how the equations are discretized and solved, how numerical errors enter the calculation, and how those choices affect the physical conclusions drawn from a simulation. What interests me is not numerical methods in isolation, but using them to make physical simulation more reliable and interpretable.

This is why Prof. Gong’s work on numerical methods for frequency-domain wave equations and antenna electromagnetics caught my attention. It is close to the class of problems I have already worked on, but approaches them at a deeper numerical level. I do not yet have a fixed thesis topic in mind. I would prefer to build the necessary numerical foundation first and then develop a more specific research question through discussion and training in the group.
