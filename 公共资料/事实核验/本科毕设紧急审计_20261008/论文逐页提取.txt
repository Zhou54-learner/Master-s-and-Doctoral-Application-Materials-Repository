I 
Abstract 
To meet the demand for lightweight and low-reflection broadband microwave absorbing 
structures in complex electromagnetic environments, this thesis designs a metal-backed absorber 
based on resistive-film metamaterials. The structure consists of a metal ground, two orthogonal PET 
substrates, and single-sided patterned resistive films, with the continuous effective bandwidth of 
𝑅𝐿< −10 dB in the 2–18 GHz range adopted as the main evaluation index. The thin-solid-film 
model and the Ohmic-sheet surface-resistance model are compared, and the Ohmic-sheet model, 
which better matches the engineering characterization of sheet-resistance materials, is selected for 
subsequent optimization. A CST-Python co-simulation and genetic-algorithm workflow is 
established to perform 6×6, 9×9, and 12×12 pixelated topology optimizations, with independent 
X/Y sheet resistances and a semi-constrained multi-template strategy introduced. The final structure 
has 𝑝 =13.1409mm, ℎ= 10.0731mm, 𝑅𝑠,𝑥= 140.79 Ω/𝑠𝑞, and 𝑅𝑠,𝑦= 35.461 Ω/sq. It achieves 
effective absorption from 4.80 to 18.00 GHz, with a bandwidth of 13.20 GHz and a coverage ratio 
of 82.5%. Field and loss analyses show that the broadband absorption mainly results from 
transmission suppression by the metal ground, spatial coupling between the X/Y-oriented resistive 
sheets, multimode responses, and surface ohmic loss. 
Keywords:  resistive film, metamaterial absorber, surface sheet resistance, broadband 
microwave absorption, genetic algorithm, CST-Python co-simulation

Chapter 1  Introduction 
1.1  Research Background and Significance 
With the rapid development of wireless communication, radar detection, satellite remote 
sensing, electronic countermeasures and intelligent equipment, electromagnetic waves have become 
important carriers for transmitting energy and information in modern information systems. At the 
same time, the number of electronic devices is increasing, their operating frequency ranges are 
continuously expanding, and electromagnetic interference, electromagnetic leakage and target 
scattering problems in complex environments have become increasingly prominent. In application 
scenarios such as electromagnetic compatibility, radar stealth, precision testing and electronic 
equipment protection, how to effectively reduce the reflection of incident electromagnetic waves 
from structural surfaces has become an important issue in the design of microwave absorbing 
materials and electromagnetic functional structures. 
Conventional microwave absorbing materials usually rely on intrinsic dielectric loss, magnetic 
loss or porous composite structures to dissipate electromagnetic energy and have a certain 
engineering application basis. However, traditional materials still have limitations in broadband 
absorption, lightweight and thin design, and structural designability. In particular, in a broad 
frequency range such as 2-18 GHz, which covers several typical microwave bands including S, C, 
X and Ku, if one relies only on material thickness and the quarter-wavelength matching principle, 
the structural thickness tends to increase significantly with the low-frequency wavelength, which is 
unfavorable for lightweight and compact design. Therefore, a broadband absorbing structure not 
only needs sufficient loss but also needs to maintain good impedance matching over a wide 
frequency range. 
Metamaterials provide a new approach for overcoming the performance limitations of 
conventional materials by regulating effective electromagnetic parameters through artificially 
designed subwavelength structural units. Since the studies on negative-index media and artificial 
electromagnetic metamaterials were proposed, metamaterials have been widely used in 
electromagnetic-wave propagation control, stealth, frequency selective surfaces and absorbers [1-4]. 
The perfect metamaterial absorber proposed by Landy et al. in 2008 demonstrated that, through the 
regulation of electric response, magnetic response and loss in artificial structures, nearly complete 
absorption can be achieved at a specific frequency [5]. Since then, metamaterial absorbers have 
gradually developed from single-frequency strong absorption toward multi-frequency, broadband, 
flexible, transparent and reconfigurable designs [6-10]. 
Among broadband microwave absorbing structures, resistive-film metamaterials have 
considerable research value. Resistive films have the characteristics of small thickness, adjustable 
sheet resistance, easy patterning and relatively flexible processing methods, and they can generate 
ohmic loss through surface currents. Compared with ideal metallic resonant units, resistive-film 
patterns can not only provide structural resonance but also directly participate in energy dissipation. 
Compared with uniform resistive sheets, patterned resistive films can further regulate the equivalent 
impedance and multimode response by changing the connectivity of conductive regions, edge

This project studies a broadband microwave absorbing structure based on resistive-film

1.2  Research Status of Metamaterial Absorbers 
The research basis of metamaterial absorbers comes from the regulation of effective 
permittivity, effective permeability and surface impedance by artificial electromagnetic structures. 
Early metamaterial research mainly focused on negative refractive index, artificial magnetic 
response and anomalous electromagnetic propagation [1-4]. In the field of absorption, typical 
metamaterial absorbers are usually composed of periodic metallic patterns, dielectric layers and 
metallic backing plates. The metallic backing plate suppresses transmission, the periodic structure 
regulates the electromagnetic response, and the dielectric layer and loss units dissipate energy. 
When the input impedance of the structure approaches the impedance of free space, the incident 
wave can be reflected less at the front surface and dissipated inside the structure by loss 
mechanisms. 
The metallic resonant perfect absorber proposed by Landy et al. proved that artificial structures 
can achieve single-frequency strong absorption through the coupling of electric and magnetic 
resonances [5]. Subsequently, researchers expanded the absorption bandwidth through multilayer 
stacking, multi-resonant units, multi-scale patterns and loading of lossy materials [6-8]. For example, 
multilayer structures can introduce multiple response channels along the thickness direction, multi-
scale patterns can make units of different sizes correspond to different absorption peaks, and 
frequency selective surfaces can regulate reflection characteristics through combinations of 
equivalent inductance, capacitance and resistance [8-10]. These studies have promoted the 
development of metamaterial absorbers from single-frequency absorption to broadband absorption. 
However, broadband absorber design still faces two main problems. First, strongly resonant 
structures usually achieve deep absorption valleys, but their bandwidth is limited; to broaden the 
bandwidth, multilayer structures or complex units often have to be introduced, increasing the 
thickness and structural complexity. Second, artificially designed patterns contain many parameters, 
and different parameters are coupled with each other, so traditional empirical tuning makes it 
difficult to efficiently find optimal structures in a large design space. For broadband absorption 
targets, structural performance should not be evaluated only by the minimum reflection loss at a 
single frequency point, but should focus more on the continuous effective bandwidth with RL<-10 
dB and the coverage ratio of the target band.

In recent years, flexible, transparent, reconfigurable and multifunctional absorbing structures 
have become research hotspots. Some studies have used flexible dielectrics, transparent conductive 
films, shape-memory materials or composite nanomaterials to combine electromagnetic absorption 
performance with structural adaptability [14-18]. These studies show that the development trend of 
absorbers is no longer limited to a single absorption performance, but is gradually turning toward 
broadband, lightweight, designable and multifunctionally integrated structures. For an 
undergraduate thesis, studying resistive-film broadband absorbing structures under controllable 
simulation conditions conforms to the current development direction of metamaterial absorbers and 
also facilitates analysis from both the physical-mechanism and optimization-method perspectives. 
1.3  Research Status of Resistive Films and Sheet-Resistance Absorbing 
Structures 
Resistive-film absorbing structures are usually composed of a resistive film, a dielectric 
supporting layer and a metallic backing plate. The key parameter of a resistive film is its sheet 
resistance, usually expressed in Ω/sq. Sheet resistance determines the magnitude of surface current 
on the film surface and the strength of energy dissipation, and it also affects the equivalent surface 
impedance of the structure. If the sheet resistance is too low, the structure approaches a conductor 
surface and the incident wave is prone to strong reflection; if the sheet resistance is too high, surface 
currents cannot be sufficiently excited and ohmic loss is insufficient. Therefore, sheet-resistance 
parameters need to be optimized together with geometric patterns and structural height. 
Compared with metallic-patch absorbers, resistive films are not ideal conductors; instead, they 
have both conductive response and loss functions. When incident electromagnetic waves act on a 
patterned resistive film, surface currents are excited on the film surface, and the currents generate 
Joule heat loss when passing through finite sheet-resistance regions, thereby dissipating 
electromagnetic energy. Patterning further enhances the designability of resistive-film structures: 
edges, openings, windows, bridges and local slits on the film surface change charge accumulation 
positions, current-path lengths and local field-enhancement regions, enabling different response 
modes to be excited in different frequency bands. 
Existing studies have shown that resistive frequency selective surfaces, resistive sheets and 
flexible conductive films can be used to construct broadband microwave absorbing structures [11-12]. 
Sheet-resistance materials represented by ITO, graphene composites, carbon-based films and 
nanocomposite conductive films can be combined with PET, PEN and polymer substrates to form 
lightweight, flexible or transparent absorbing structures [14-18]. Such structures have potential 
applications in stealth, electromagnetic compatibility and flexible electronics. 
Although resistive-film absorbing structures have obvious advantages, their design still has 
difficulties. On the one hand, continuous uniform resistive films have limited adjustable degrees of 
freedom and are difficult to use to obtain stable low reflection over the 2-18 GHz broadband. On the 
other hand, if the resistive film is discretized into a high-resolution pixelated pattern, the number of 
design variables increases rapidly, manual parameter sweeping becomes inefficient, and complex 
patterns are prone to fragmentation and unclear physical meaning. Therefore, the key to broadband 
resistive-film absorber design is not only selecting an appropriate sheet resistance, but also 
establishing a reasonable pattern-encoding method and optimization search approach.

1.4  Application of Numerical Simulation and Intelligent Optimization 
Methods in Absorbing Structure Design 
The performance of microwave absorbing structures usually needs to be obtained through full-
wave electromagnetic simulation. For periodic metamaterial units, periodic boundaries and Floquet 
ports can be used so that a single periodic unit approximates an infinite periodic array, thereby 
calculating the reflection coefficient, field distribution and loss distribution under plane-wave 
incidence. Commercial electromagnetic simulation software such as CST Studio Suite can handle 
complex three-dimensional structures and periodic-boundary problems well, and is therefore 
commonly used for metamaterial absorber design and performance analysis [20]. 
However, when the structural pattern is discretized into a pixel matrix, the optimization space 
increases sharply. Taking a 12×12 pattern as an example, if each pixel can independently be 
retained or removed, the theoretical number of combinations is extremely large. If X/Y two 
orthogonal film surfaces, the structural period, height and different sheet-resistance parameters are 
also considered, the design space further expands. Traditional manual tuning is difficult to use for 
this mixed problem of discrete topology variables and continuous geometric variables. 
A genetic algorithm is a typical population-based intelligent optimization method. It does not 
rely on gradients, is suitable for discrete encoding and can be readily linked with simulation 
software [19]. In electromagnetic-structure optimization, a genetic algorithm can encode candidate 
structures as individuals, evaluate their absorption performance through a fitness function, and then 
improve the population generation by generation through selection, crossover, mutation and elitism. 
For the pixelated resistive-film patterns studied in this thesis, each pixel can be represented by 0 or 
1 to indicate whether the sheet-resistance region is retained, making the problem naturally suitable 
for genetic-algorithm search. 
This thesis adopts a CST-Python co-simulation optimization workflow. Python is responsible 
for generating structural parameters, decoding pixelated patterns, calling CST for modeling and 
solving, reading S11 data and calculating reflection loss. CST is responsible for three-dimensional 
modeling and frequency-domain electromagnetic solving of the periodic unit. This workflow can 
reduce repetitive manual modeling work and allows structural optimization at different stages to be 
compared under unified simulation conditions. Compared with purely manual tuning, automatic 
optimization can explore the design space of broadband absorbing structures more systematically. 
1.5  Main Research Contents 
This thesis studies the design and optimization of a broadband microwave absorbing structure 
based on resistive-film metamaterials. The main work is as follows. 
(1) A resistive-film metamaterial absorbing unit model is established. This thesis constructs a 
periodic absorbing unit consisting of a metallic backing plate, two orthogonal PET substrates and 
single-sided patterned resistive films. The metallic backing plate is used to suppress transmission, 
the PET substrates support the resistive films and form an orthogonal spatial structure, and the 
patterned resistive films regulate surface-current paths and generate ohmic loss. Periodic boundaries 
and a Floquet port are adopted in the simulation to study the reflection-loss characteristics in the 2-
18 GHz band.

1.6  Summary of This Chapter 
This chapter introduces the research background and significance of broadband microwave 
absorbing structures, and summarizes the development status of metamaterial absorbers, resistive-
film sheet-resistance absorbing structures and numerical-simulation optimization methods. 
Conventional absorbing materials and single-resonance metamaterial structures still have certain 
limitations in broadband, lightweight and high-degree-of-freedom design, while patterned resistive-
film metamaterials can simultaneously utilize surface-current regulation, ohmic loss and multimode 
coupling to provide an effective solution for broadband microwave absorption. To address the 
difficulty of manually designing high-degree-of-freedom pixelated patterns, this thesis adopts CST-
Python co-simulation and a genetic algorithm for staged optimization, and the subsequent chapters 
introduce the theoretical basis, modeling method, optimization process and final structural 
mechanism analysis.

Chapter 2  Theoretical Basis of Resistive-Film Metamaterial

2.1  Electromagnetic-Wave Absorption and Reflection Loss 
The essential task of a microwave absorbing structure is to reduce the reflection of incident 
electromagnetic waves as much as possible over a certain frequency range and to effectively couple 
the incident energy into the structure, where it is converted into other forms of energy through 
mechanisms such as dielectric loss, magnetic loss or ohmic loss. For a general electromagnetic 
structure, the incident power can be divided into reflected power, transmitted power and absorbed 
power. Therefore, the absorptivity A can be expressed as 
𝐴= 1 −𝑅−𝑇                                                   (2-1) 
where 𝑅 is the reflectance and 𝑇 is the transmittance. If the structure is characterized by 
scattering parameters, then 
𝑅= |𝑆11|2,   𝑇= |𝑆21|2                                        (2-2) 
Therefore, the absorptivity can also be written as 
𝐴= 1 −|𝑆11|2 −|𝑆21|2                                            (2-3) 
In practical microwave-absorber research, reflection characteristics are usually of greater 
concern. Therefore, reflection loss (RL) is often used to evaluate absorption performance, and it is 
defined as 
𝑅𝐿= 20log10|𝑆11|                                                (2-4) 
Since |𝑆11| is usually smaller than 1, 𝑅𝐿 is generally negative. The smaller its numerical value, 
the weaker the reflection and the stronger the absorption. For example, when 𝑅𝐿= −10 𝑑𝐵, the 
reflected power is only 10% of the incident power, and the structure is usually considered to possess 
effective absorption capability; when 𝑅𝐿= −20 𝑑𝐵, the reflected power is only 1%, indicating 
further enhanced absorption. 
Compared with a simple absorptivity curve, the reflection-loss curve is more suitable for 
engineering analysis because it can intuitively reflect the low-reflection intervals, absorption-valley 
positions and broadband continuity of the structure at different frequencies. For broadband 
absorption design, it is insufficient to focus only on whether a deep absorption valley appears at a 
certain frequency. More importantly, one should determine whether there is a sufficiently wide 
continuous interval below -10 dB in the target band. Therefore, the continuous effective absorption 
bandwidth and the coverage ratio in the 2-18 GHz band are used as the main evaluation indices in 
later chapters, rather than taking the minimum reflection loss as the sole criterion.

2.2  Metal-Backed Absorbing Structures and the Principle of Impedance 
Matching 
Among metamaterial absorbers, a common type is the metal-backed absorber. Such structures 
usually consist of a functional surface layer, a dielectric supporting layer and a metallic backing 
plate. Because the bottom metallic backing plate can be approximated as a perfect conductor, 
incident electromagnetic waves cannot penetrate the structure, and the transmission term can be 
approximately regarded as zero, namely 
𝑇≈0 
Thus, the absorptivity can be simplified as  
𝐴= 1 −𝑅= 1 −|𝑆11|2                                         (2-5) 
This means that, for a metal-backed absorbing structure, the overall absorptivity can be 
effectively improved as long as the front-surface reflection is reduced. Therefore, the design focus 
of such structures is not transmission suppression, but reflection reduction and internal-loss 
enhancement. 
From the perspective of electromagnetic-wave incidence, the strength of front-surface 
reflection mainly depends on the degree of matching between the structural input impedance Zin 
and the free-space impedance 𝑍0. If the two differ greatly, the incident wave will be significantly 
reflected at the interface; if they are close to each other, the incident wave can more easily enter the 
structure. The reflection coefficient can be written as

In other words, impedance matching is the primary condition for achieving low reflection. 
However, impedance matching alone is not sufficient to guarantee good absorption. If the 
internal loss of the structure is insufficient, even if the electromagnetic wave is successfully coupled 
into the structure, it may leak out again after multiple internal reflections. Therefore, an ideal 
absorbing structure must satisfy two conditions simultaneously: first, good impedance matching at 
the interface so that the incident wave can enter the structure; second, sufficient internal energy 
dissipation so that the electromagnetic energy entering the structure is rapidly converted into heat or 
other loss forms. For the resistive-film metamaterial studied in this thesis, the former is mainly 
regulated by the geometric pattern, structural height and double-orthogonal spatial layout, while the 
latter mainly relies on ohmic loss on the surface of the resistive film. 
The metallic backing plate also plays another important role in this process, namely forming 
equivalent current loops and magnetic response regions with the upper patterned layer. Under 
incident-wave excitation, currents are excited on the patterned layer surface, while image currents 
form on the metallic backing plate. The coupling between them changes the equivalent impedance 
of the structure and enhances the local field response. This is also one important reason why 
metamaterial absorbers can obtain good absorption performance under relatively small thickness 
conditions.

are not fully equivalent in mesh implementation and boundary approximation. Therefore, after

turns to the Ohmic Sheet model for 6×6, 9×9 and 12×12 high-resolution topology optimization, 
making the model closer to the practical description of sheet-resistance materials.

2.5  Electromagnetic Regulation Mechanism of Patterned Resistive Films 
A uniform resistive film has a certain surface-loss capability, but because its geometric degrees 
of freedom are limited, its equivalent impedance and resonance characteristics have little room for 
adjustment, making it difficult to maintain continuous low reflection over a wide band. After the 
resistive film is patterned, the electromagnetic regulation capability of the structure is significantly 
enhanced. 
The function of a patterned resistive film is mainly reflected in the following aspects. First, 
retaining and removing different conductive regions changes the surface-current paths. Longer 
continuous conductive paths are more favorable for low-frequency response because low-frequency 
electromagnetic waves correspond to larger equivalent loop sizes. Shorter local edges, openings or 
isolated compensation regions are more likely to participate in high-frequency local responses. 
Second, pattern edges and window regions produce local charge accumulation and strong electric-
field enhancement, thereby affecting local inductance, capacitance and equivalent impedance. Third, 
coupling between different regions allows the structure to produce different response modes near 
multiple frequencies, forming multiple absorption peaks that connect with each other in the 
frequency domain and ultimately forming a relatively wide effective absorption bandwidth. 
For the orthogonal double-film structure in this thesis, the X-oriented and Y-oriented resistive 
films do not perform exactly the same function. Generally, the layer with more continuous retained 
regions and lower sheet resistance more easily forms the main dissipation channel and strongly 


=== PDF PAGE 17 ===
 
12 
affects the low-frequency absorption boundary. The layer with more local retained regions and 
higher sheet resistance is more suitable as an impedance-regulation layer or a mid-to-high-
frequency compensation layer. The two layers are orthogonally arranged in space, so that the 
structure has not only two-dimensional pattern degrees of freedom but also additional coupling 
paths in three-dimensional space, thereby increasing the design freedom. 
In terms of mechanism, broadband absorption is usually not produced by a single resonance 
mode, but by the combined action of multiple modes. Some modes may mainly push the low-
frequency absorption boundary downward, some enhance high-frequency continuity, and others 
serve as bridges to smooth the transition between different absorption valleys. Therefore, pixelated 
patterns have significant advantages in broadband absorber design, because they can flexibly 
control edge number, connectivity and local coupling strength through discrete retaining/removing 
strategies.

2.6  Basic Principles of Pixelated Topology and Genetic-Algorithm 
Optimization 
After the resistive film is discretized into a pixel matrix, each pixel can be regarded as a binary 
variable, namely retaining or removing the sheet-resistance region. If a film surface contains 𝑚× 𝑛 
pixels, then this film surface alone theoretically has 2𝑚𝑛 possible pattern combinations. If X/Y two 
film surfaces are patterned simultaneously and continuous variables such as 𝑝, ℎ, 𝑅𝑠,𝑥 and 𝑅𝑠,𝑦 are 
optimized together, the overall design space becomes extremely large. For such problems, relying 
only on empirical parameter sweeps is often inefficient and cannot guarantee a relatively optimal 
result. 


=== PDF PAGE 18 ===
 
13 
A genetic algorithm (GA) is a population-based intelligent optimization method that simulates 
natural selection and biological evolution [19]. Its basic idea is as follows: first, several candidate 
structures are randomly generated as the initial population; then, each individual is evaluated 
according to a fitness function; next, a new generation is produced through selection, crossover and 
mutation so that high-fitness features are retained and propagated; after multiple generations of 
evolution, the overall population performance gradually improves and tends to converge. 
In this study, pixelated patterns are well suited to genetic-algorithm encoding. Each pixel can 
be represented by a binary variable, for example 1 indicating that the sheet-resistance region is 
retained and 0 indicating that it is removed. Continuous variables such as structural period, height 
and sheet resistance can be handled through real-number encoding or discrete-grid mapping. In this 
way, the entire absorbing structure can be represented as a chromosome and input into CST through 
an automatic workflow for full-wave simulation. 
The advantages of the genetic algorithm in this project are mainly reflected in three aspects. 
First, it does not rely on gradient information and is suitable for complex optimization problems 
involving mixed discrete topology variables and continuous variables. Second, it can perform a 
global search over a large design space and avoid premature trapping in local optima. Third, it can 
be easily combined with the Python-CST co-simulation workflow to realize an integrated closed-
loop process of structure generation, simulation solving, result extraction and fitness updating. 
Of course, genetic algorithms also require large computational cost, especially when each 
candidate structure must undergo a full-wave simulation. Therefore, in practical implementation, 
structural pre-screening, pattern constraints, template restrictions or elite preservation are usually 
introduced to reduce invalid computations and improve search efficiency. The staged optimization 
route, semi-constrained multi-template method and pattern-connectivity constraints used in later 
chapters are developed on this basis.

3.1  Structural Design Concept 
The research object of this thesis is a broadband microwave absorbing structure based on 
resistive-film metamaterials. Its basic objective is to obtain the widest possible continuous low-
reflection bandwidth in the 2-18 GHz band. According to the theoretical analysis above, the 
performance of a metal-backed absorbing structure mainly depends on two aspects: first, the 
matching degree between the structural input impedance and free-space impedance; second, the 
ability of the structure to dissipate incident electromagnetic energy. If impedance matching is 
insufficient, the incident wave is strongly reflected at the front surface. If loss is insufficient, even if 
the wave enters the structure, it cannot be fully dissipated. Therefore, broadband absorber design 
must consider both impedance regulation and energy loss. 
Conventional metallic resonant metamaterial absorbers usually rely on localized resonance 
among metallic patterns, dielectric layers and metallic backing plates to achieve strong absorption 
near a certain frequency point. Such structures can have deep absorption peaks, but their bandwidth 
is often limited. To obtain a wider absorption band, this thesis adopts the resistive film as the main 
functional layer. A resistive film has a certain surface sheet resistance; it can generate surface 
currents under incident electromagnetic waves and convert electromagnetic energy into ohmic loss 
through finite resistance. Compared with ideal metallic patches, resistive films not only regulate 
electromagnetic-field distribution but also directly participate in energy dissipation. 
In terms of structure, this thesis does not use a single-layer planar resistive film, but constructs 
a spatial cross structure composed of two orthogonal PET substrates and patterned resistive films. 
This structure is equivalent to introducing two regulation channels, the X direction and the Y 
direction, within one periodic unit, enriching surface-current paths, local field-enhancement regions 
and spatial coupling modes. By tuning the bidirectional resistive-film patterns, sheet-resistance 
parameters, period size and structural height, different local response modes can be excited in 
different frequency bands, allowing multiple absorption intervals to connect in the frequency 
domain and thereby improving the overall broadband absorption performance. 
The modeling concept of this thesis can be summarized as follows. First, a metal-backed 
orthogonal resistive-film absorbing unit model is established, and the material parameters, 
geometric parameters, boundary conditions and port settings are clarified. Then, the differences 
between the thin solid resistive film model and the Ohmic Sheet surface sheet-resistance model are 
compared, and the unified modeling method for subsequent high-resolution topology optimization 
is determined. Finally, the resistive-film pattern is discretized into a pixel matrix, providing a 
parametric model basis for the CST-Python co-simulation and genetic-algorithm optimization in 
Chapter 4.

This thesis adopts a periodic-unit model to study the electromagnetic response of the absorbing

resistance. Both methods can describe resistive-film loss in electromagnetic simulation, but they 
differ significantly in physical assumptions, parameter input method and computational cost. 
In the thin solid film model, the resistive film is modeled as a three-dimensional thin layer with 
finite thickness t and volume conductivity sigma. The relationship among sheet resistance 𝑅𝑠,

3.5.3  Cross-Sectional Characteristics of Engineering Resistive-Film Materials 
Common resistive-film materials in engineering usually have a layered structure that can be 
summarized as an ultrathin conductive/resistive coating plus a polymer substrate. The conductive 
layer may consist of carbon-based materials, transparent conductive materials, conductive particle 
networks or printed resistive coatings, while the supporting layer is usually PET or another polymer 
film. Compared with the operating wavelength of electromagnetic waves, the conductive layer is 
extremely thin, and its microscopic thickness, conductive network and local uniformity are often 
difficult to accurately describe using an ideal uniform volume conductor. 
Figure 3-5 shows the typical layered cross-section of a sheet-resistance resistive-film material

input material parameters into the simulation model without artificially assuming an ideal uniform 
thickness and volume conductivity.

3.5.4  Basis for the Final Modeling Choice 
Based on the above analysis, this thesis finally selects the Ohmic Sheet model as the unified 
modeling method for subsequent 6×6, 9×9 and 12×12 topology optimization. This choice is mainly 
based on the following reasons. 
First, the Ohmic Sheet model is more consistent with the engineering characterization method 
of actual resistive-film materials. Actual sheet-resistance film materials usually directly provide 𝑅𝑠 
parameters, and the Ohmic Sheet model avoids forcibly converting sheet resistance into volume 
conductivity and an assumed thickness. 
Second, the Ohmic Sheet model is more suitable for high-resolution topology optimization. As 
the pattern resolution increases from 6×6 to 9×9 and then to 12×12, the film surface is divided into 
more and more pixel regions. If every retained pixel is modeled as a thin solid with finite thickness, 
the number of mesh cells and solving time will increase significantly, which is unfavorable for 
evaluating a large number of candidate structures in the genetic algorithm. 
Third, the two models maintain consistent overall response trends. Although the thin solid film 
model obtains a deeper absorption valley and wider effective bandwidth in the early baseline 
structure, the Ohmic Sheet model can still reflect the main absorption characteristics of the structure 
and is more suitable as an approximate model of an engineering sheet-resistance structure. 
Fourth, the Ohmic Sheet model facilitates the subsequent introduction of X/Y layered sheet 
resistance. In the final 12×12 structure, the X-oriented and Y-oriented film surfaces have different 
sheet resistances and respectively undertake impedance regulation and main loss functions. The

as the final modeling basis. Instead, from the perspectives of engineering material characterization, 
automatic optimization efficiency, physical meaning of parameters and model consistency, the 
Ohmic Sheet surface sheet-resistance model is selected as the basis for subsequent research.

3.6  Pixelated Topology Construction and Parametric Modeling Method 
After determining the Ohmic Sheet model as the unified modeling method, this thesis further 
discretizes the resistive-film pattern into a pixelated topology. The basic idea of the pixelated 
method is to divide the continuous film surface into a regular grid, and use a binary variable to 
represent the state of each pixel: 1 means that the resistive film is retained in that region, and 0 
means that the resistive film is removed. By changing the distribution of 0 and 1 in the pixel matrix, 
different resistive-film patterns can be obtained. 
This thesis adopts three pattern resolutions in stages: 6×6, 9×9 and 12×12. The 6×6 stage is 
mainly used to verify the feasibility of the automatic optimization workflow. The 9×9 stage further 
increases pattern freedom and enriches local edges, windows and connection regions. The 12×12 
stage is used for final high-resolution topology optimization, enabling the structure to regulate both 
low-frequency long-path responses and high-frequency local compensation modes. 
Pixelated topology has two main advantages. First, it can be readily combined with a genetic 
algorithm. Each pixel state can be directly used as a binary gene, and the entire pattern matrix can 
be unfolded into a chromosome for selection, crossover and mutation operations. Second, it can 


=== PDF PAGE 31 ===
 
26 
express complex patterns in a relatively simple way. Compared with manually designing several 
limited regular shapes, pixelated topology allows the algorithm to automatically search for more 
complex edges, windows, bridges and local compensation regions. 
However, high-resolution pixelation also causes the design space to increase rapidly. For 
example, a 12×12 single-layer pattern theoretically contains 144 pixels; if the X/Y two layers vary 
independently at the same time, the search space becomes very large. Therefore, this thesis 
combines central symmetry, connectivity checks, template constraints and a semi-constrained multi-
template strategy in subsequent optimization to reduce obviously invalid or fragmented structures 
and improve search efficiency and result interpretability. 
In the final 12×12 structure, the X-oriented film surface has fewer retained pixels and mainly 
forms a central transverse conductive path and local compensation regions. The Y-oriented film 
surface has more retained pixels and forms an outer frame, windows and continuous channel 
structures. This indicates that pixelated optimization changes not only the film area but, more 
importantly, the current path, local field-enhancement positions and distribution of dissipation 
regions.

4.1  Overall Concept of Automatic Optimization 
In Chapter 3, the three-dimensional simulation model of the resistive-film metamaterial 
absorbing unit has been established, and the Ohmic Sheet surface sheet-resistance model has been 
determined as the unified modeling method for subsequent topology optimization. Because the 
research object of this thesis simultaneously contains continuous geometric parameters, continuous 
sheet-resistance parameters and discrete pixelated topology variables, relying completely on manual 
modeling and parameter sweeping would require heavy workload and would make it difficult to 
systematically find a better structure in a high-dimensional design space. Therefore, this thesis 
further constructs a CST-Python co-simulation and genetic-algorithm optimization workflow to 
realize automatic modeling, automatic solving, automatic evaluation and generation-by-generation 
optimization of the absorbing structure. 
The core objective of the optimization problem in this thesis is to obtain the widest possible 
continuous effective absorption bandwidth below -10 dB in the 2-18 GHz band. Compared with 
merely pursuing the minimum reflection loss at a single frequency point, this thesis pays more 
attention to the continuity and stability of the reflection-loss curve over a broadband range. 
Therefore, in the automatic optimization process, the fitness evaluation is mainly based on 
continuous effective bandwidth and target-band coverage ratio, while also considering absorption 
depth and curve margin. 
The overall workflow can be summarized as follows. First, Python generates the geometric 
parameters, sheet-resistance parameters and X/Y bidirectional resistive-film topology patterns of a 
candidate structure. Then CST automatically establishes the periodic-unit model and performs 
frequency-domain solving. After the simulation, S11 data are exported and post-processed by 
Python to obtain the reflection-loss curve, effective bandwidth and coverage ratio. Finally, 
according to the fitness results, the genetic algorithm performs selection, crossover, mutation and 
elite preservation on the population to generate a new generation of candidate structures. After 
multiple generations of iteration, structural performance gradually improves and the optimized 
structure satisfying the broadband absorption target is obtained. 
This automatic workflow has two advantages. First, it can reduce a large amount of repetitive 
manual modeling and parameter modification, allowing different candidate structures to be 
compared under unified simulation conditions. Second, it can handle high-degree-of-freedom 
discrete topology search problems, especially the 6×6, 9×9 and 12×12 pixelated resistive-film 
pattern optimization tasks in this thesis. The genetic algorithm does not rely on gradient information 
and is suitable for nonlinear optimization problems involving mixed discrete and continuous 
variables, so it is used in the topology optimization workflow of this thesis [19].

4.5  Genetic Operations and Population Updating Mechanism 
This thesis uses a genetic algorithm to optimize candidate structures generation by generation. 
Each generation consists of several candidate individuals, and each individual corresponds to a 
complete absorbing structure, including geometric parameters, sheet-resistance parameters and X/Y 
pattern topology. After CST simulation and Python post-processing, each individual obtains a 
fitness value. The algorithm then updates the population according to fitness so that the features of 
better structures are retained and propagated to the next generation. 
The genetic operations mainly include selection, crossover, mutation and elite preservation. 
Selection chooses individuals with better performance from the current population as parents, 
making favorable features easier to inherit. Crossover exchanges some gene segments of two parent 
individuals to generate new combined structures. Mutation randomly changes some pixel states or 
parameter values to maintain population diversity and prevent the algorithm from prematurely 
falling into a local optimum. Elite preservation ensures that the current best individual is not lost 
during random updating. 
In resistive-film topology optimization, crossover and mutation have a large influence on the 
search result. If the mutation probability is too low, the population may quickly concentrate in a 
local region and have difficulty exploring new patterns. If the mutation probability is too high, good 
topology structures may be excessively destroyed and convergence becomes difficult. Therefore, 
this thesis reasonably controls the strength of genetic operations at different stages according to the 
size of the search space and pattern resolution. For the 12×12 high-resolution pattern, a semi-
constrained multi-template strategy is further introduced so that the search process has both free-
evolution ability and the ability to retain known favorable low-frequency skeleton and high-
frequency compensation features.

4.6  Staged Optimization Strategy 
Directly performing a completely random search in the 12×12 high-resolution topology space

high computational cost. Therefore, this thesis does not complete the final optimization in one step, 
but adopts a staged optimization strategy from low freedom to high freedom and from coarse 
resolution to fine resolution. 
First, in the 6×6 stage, a lower-resolution pattern is used for preliminary topology exploration. 
This stage has lower pattern freedom and relatively small computational cost, so it is suitable for 
verifying the feasibility of the CST-Python co-simulation workflow and the genetic-algorithm 
optimization framework. Through this stage, it is possible to preliminarily determine whether the

pattern that can serve as a reference for subsequent optimization.

optimization proceeds to the 9×9 high-resolution topology stage. Compared with the 6×6 stage, the 
9×9 stage has more local edges, windows and connection regions, allowing more detailed regulation 
of current paths and local field-enhancement positions. Then, based on a fixed superior 9×9 
topology, the period p and height h are slightly perturbed and optimized to analyze the influence of 
geometric dimensions on the reflection-loss curve. 
Finally, in the 12×12 stage, higher-resolution pixel patterns, X/Y layered sheet resistance and a 
semi-constrained multi-template strategy are introduced. This stage is not a completely random 
search; instead, it allows local evolution on the basis of existing favorable structures. Low-

templates work together so that the structure can consider the low-frequency boundary, mid-to-

5.1  Overall Concept of Staged Optimization 
Based on the Ohmic Sheet surface sheet-resistance model established above and the CST-
Python co-simulation and genetic-algorithm optimization workflow, this chapter focuses on the 
topology optimization results at different stages and the corresponding changes in absorption 
performance. Because the structure studied in this thesis contains several types of variables, 
including period, height, sheet resistance and pixelated pattern, directly conducting a completely 
random search in the high-resolution 12×12 pattern space would require high computational cost 
and would easily generate many fragmented, invalid or difficult-to-interpret patterns. Therefore, this 
thesis adopts a staged optimization strategy from low freedom to high freedom and from coarse 
resolution to fine resolution. 
The overall optimization route can be summarized as follows. First, the 6×6 thin solid film 
model is used to verify the feasibility of the double-orthogonal resistive-film structure and the 
automatic optimization workflow. Then the modeling method is changed to the Ohmic Sheet 
surface sheet-resistance model, and 6×6 pattern optimization is performed again under this model. 
Next, the pattern resolution is increased to 9×9 so that finer local structures can improve the 
broadband absorption performance under the sheet-resistance model. Finally, the resolution is 
further increased to 12×12, and X/Y layered sheet resistance and a semi-constrained multi-template 
optimization strategy are introduced to obtain the final broadband absorbing structure. 
The analysis in this chapter does not simply compare which stage has the deepest reflection-
loss valley, but focuses on the evolution of continuous effective bandwidth, low-frequency starting 
boundary and band coverage ratio. This is because the design objective of this thesis is broadband 
effective absorption in the 2-18 GHz range; an isolated deep absorption valley cannot represent 
overall broadband performance. Therefore, the final evaluation still uses the continuous effective 
bandwidth satisfying 𝑅𝐿< −10 𝑑𝐵 and the coverage ratio as the main criteria. 
5.2  Baseline Optimization Results of the 6×6 Thin Solid Film Model 
In the preliminary modeling stage, a thin solid film model is first adopted to optimize the 6×6 
pixelated resistive film structure. The main purpose of this stage is not to obtain the final structure, 
but to verify the feasibility of the orthogonal resistive film absorbing unit, the CST–Python 
automated modeling workflow, and the genetic algorithm search strategy. Because the 6×6 pattern 
has a relatively low resolution, each single-layer film contains only 36 pixels, yielding a rather 
small design space that is suitable for early-stage coarse searching and method validation. 
The baseline 6×6 thin solid film structure utilizes two orthogonal film planes (X and Y) to 
jointly control the absorption. The genetic algorithm adjusts the pixel retention states on the film 
surfaces together with the geometric parameters of the structure, so that the reflection loss (RL) 
curve remains below −10 dB over a wide frequency band. The optimal result at this stage achieves 
RL < −10 dB from 5.12 GHz to 18.00 GHz, corresponding to a continuous effective absorption

minimum reflection loss of −39.671 dB.

As can be seen from Figure 5‑1, the 6×6 thin solid film structure is already capable of

resistive films can realize a broadband low-reflection response through patterning; second, the 
combination of CST–Python and a genetic algorithm can automatically search for structures with

feasibility of the structure” and “verifying the feasibility of the optimization workflow.” 
It should be noted that, although the thin solid film model yields a deep absorption valley 
and a wide effective bandwidth at this stage, its modeling relies on a combination of the resistive 
film thickness and the bulk conductivity. For practical engineering applications where resistive film 
materials are characterized by sheet resistance, the Ohmic Sheet model provides a more appropriate 
description of the material. Therefore, the subsequent optimization does not directly continue with 
the thin solid film model; instead, topology optimization is restarted under a unified Ohmic Sheet 
surface resistance model.

5.6  12×12 Semi‑Constrained Multi‑Template Topology Optimization

Instead, it allows local regions to continue evolving while retaining the already favorable structural 
skeletons. In this way, the low‑frequency long‑path and medium‑/high‑frequency compensation

The semi‑constrained multi‑template strategy mainly involves three functional orientations: the

larger‑scale connected regions; the second is a high‑frequency compensation template, used to add 
local edges, short paths, and small‑scale response regions; the third is a bridging/flattening template, 
used to improve the frequency‑domain joining between different absorption valleys, making the 
reflection loss curve more continuous within the effective band.

rather, it adjusts the responses in different frequency bands through the functional division between

5.9  Summary of This Chapter 
This chapter systematically analyzed the staged topology optimization results of the 
resistive‑film metamaterial absorber. First, the 6×6 thin solid film baseline optimization verified the 
feasibility of the dual‑orthogonal resistive film structure and the CST–Python genetic algorithm 
optimization workflow. Subsequently, 6×6 re‑optimization was carried out under the Ohmic Sheet 
surface‑resistance model, confirming the importance of a unified sheet‑resistance modeling 
approach. Although the 6×6 Ohmic Sheet stage could achieve effective absorption in the

the limited degrees of freedom of the low‑resolution pattern.

windows, and connection structures can effectively enhance the broadband absorption performance.

room for further optimization of the low‑frequency boundary.

structure with p = 13.1409 mm, h = 10.0731 mm, R{s,x} = 140.79 Ω/sq, and R{s,y} = 35.461 Ω/sq.

absorption bandwidth of 13.20 GHz, a coverage of 82.5%, and a minimum reflection loss of 
−23.485 dB. 
Based on the results of each stage, it can be concluded that the final performance improvement 
comes mainly from three aspects: first, the Ohmic Sheet model makes the sheet‑resistance 
parameters more consistent with the engineering characterization of the material; second, the 
increase in pattern resolution enhances the ability to control local edges, windows, and current paths;

6.2  Three-Dimensional Model and Spatial Coupling Characteristics of the

The final absorbing unit consists of three parts: a bottom metal backplane, two orthogonal PET 
substrates, and patterned Ohmic Sheet resistive films loaded on the PET surfaces. The metal 
backplane is located at the bottom of the structure to suppress transmission; the PET substrates are 
arranged along two mutually perpendicular directions, so that the X‑directed and Y‑directed 
resistive films form an orthogonal coupling in space. The patterned resistive films serve as the main 
functional layer, simultaneously undertaking surface current regulation and ohmic loss.

between the metal backplane and the resistive films above, allowing the interior of the structure to

and fewer retained areas, whereas the Y‑directed film plane has a lower sheet resistance and more 
retained areas, indicating that the two film planes are not simple repetitions but instead form a clear 
functional division. 
From the perspective of structural function, the Y‑directed low‑sheet‑resistance film plane 
more easily forms a continuous current dissipation path and is the main ohmic loss channel; the 
X‑directed higher‑sheet‑resistance film plane adjusts the impedance matching through local strips 
and compensation regions and plays a compensating role for the mid‑ and high‑frequency responses. 
Together, they enable the final structure to maintain a low reflection over a wide frequency band.

to continue maintaining effective absorption at high frequencies without obvious reflection 
enhancement in the mid-to-high-frequency range. 
By combining the electric-field distributions at the three frequencies, it can be seen that the 
main response regions of the final structure are not identical at different frequencies. The 8.8 GHz 
response relies more on larger-scale conducting paths and overall coupling; 12.0 GHz shows a 
transition response involving multiple local modes; and 15.5 GHz relies more on local edges and 
high-frequency compensation structures. This indicates that the final broadband absorption 
originates from the continuous connection of multiple electric-response regions in the frequency 
domain. 
6.4  Analysis of Magnetic Field Distribution 
The magnetic-field distribution can reflect the magnetic coupling characteristics between the 
resistive film and the metallic backing plate. For a metal-backed metamaterial absorbing structure, 
the surface current on the patterned layer and the image current in the metallic backing plate can 
form an equivalent current loop, thereby producing a magnetic response. This magnetic response 
helps regulate the input impedance of the structure so that it approaches free-space impedance 
within a certain frequency range, thereby reducing front-surface reflection.

6.5  Analysis of Power Loss Density

resistance dissipates the current energy in the form of ohmic loss. Therefore, if the power-loss 
density is mainly concentrated in the patterned resistive-film regions, especially near edges, 
windows, bridges and continuous channels, it indicates that the patterned sheet-resistance film is 
indeed the main absorbing functional layer.

6.6  Functional Division of the X/Y Orthogonal Sheet-Resistance Films 
In the final 12×12 structure, the X-direction sheet resistance is 140.79 Ω/sq and the Y-direction 
sheet resistance is 35.461 Ω/sq, showing a large difference. At the same time, the retained-pixel 
number and connectivity mode of the X- and Y-oriented patterns are also obviously different. This

indicates that the final structure is not a simple superposition of two identical film surfaces, but a 
double-layer orthogonal system with functional division. 
From the topology morphology, the Y-oriented film surface has a relatively complete outer 
frame, windows and continuous channel structures, and its sheet resistance is lower, making it 
easier to form strong surface currents and main ohmic loss. Therefore, the Y-oriented film surface 
can be understood as the main dissipation layer and low-frequency skeleton layer. Its continuous 
conducting paths help increase the equivalent current-path length and thereby push the effective 
absorption band toward lower frequencies. 
The X-oriented film surface has relatively fewer retained regions and higher sheet resistance, 
and is mainly composed of a central strip-like region and local compensation structures. Although 
this film surface does not necessarily undertake the main energy dissipation, it can improve the 
overall reflection-loss curve by changing the local electric-field distribution, regulating the spatial 
coupling state and compensating mid-to-high-frequency impedance mismatch. Therefore, the X-
oriented film surface tends to act as an impedance-regulation layer and high-frequency 
compensation layer. 
Precisely because the sheet resistance, topology and spatial direction of the X/Y two film 
surfaces are different, the final structure can excite different response modes at different frequencies. 
The low-to-mid-frequency band mainly relies on longer paths and overall coupling, the high-
frequency band mainly relies on local edges and compensation structures, and the mid-frequency 
band is connected by multiple modes. This functional division is one important reason why the final 
structure achieves continuous effective absorption over 4.80-18.00 GHz. 
6.7  Summary of the Broadband Absorption Mechanism 
By combining the reflection-loss curve, topology, electric-field distribution, magnetic-field 
distribution and surface power-loss-density distribution, the broadband absorption mechanism of 
the final 12×12 semi-constrained multi-template structure can be summarized as follows. 
First, the metallic backing plate suppresses transmission, preventing incident electromagnetic 
waves from penetrating the structure, so the absorption performance is mainly determined by front-
surface reflection. Therefore, as long as the structure can reduce S11, the overall absorptivity can be 
effectively improved. 
Second, the X/Y two layers of resistive films on the orthogonal PET substrates form a spatial 
coupling structure. Compared with a single planar film, the double-orthogonal structure provides 
more current paths and coupling modes, allowing surface currents in different directions to jointly 
participate in absorption. 
Third, the X/Y layered sheet resistance produces functional division between the two film 
surfaces. The Y-oriented low-sheet-resistance film surface mainly undertakes ohmic dissipation and 
low-frequency path construction, while the X-oriented high-sheet-resistance film surface mainly 
undertakes impedance regulation and local compensation. 
Fourth, the 12×12 pixelated pattern provides rich edges, windows, bridges and local 
compensation structures. These structures excite different local modes in different frequency bands, 
connecting multiple absorption peaks or absorption intervals in the frequency domain and thereby 
forming a broadband low-reflection response.

This chapter analyzes the absorption performance and mechanism of the final 12×12 semi-
constrained multi-template Ohmic Sheet structure. The final structure satisfies RL<-10 dB over

15.5 GHz show that the main response regions excited by the final structure are different in 
different bands. The mid-low-frequency band depends more on longer current paths and overall

the high-frequency band relies more on local edges and short-path compensation structures. 
The magnetic-field distribution shows obvious spatial coupling between the resistive film and 
the metallic backing plate. The metallic backing plate not only suppresses transmission, but also 
participates in the formation of equivalent current loops and magnetic response, helping regulate the 
input impedance of the structure. The surface power-loss-density distribution further indicates that 
the main energy dissipation is concentrated at the edges, windows and connection regions of the 
patterned sheet-resistance film, demonstrating that ohmic loss in the Ohmic Sheet resistive film is 
the main dissipation mechanism of the final absorbing structure.

Conclusions 
This thesis focused on the design of broadband microwave absorbing structures based on 
resistive-film metamaterials. A dual-orthogonal absorbing unit model consisting of a metal 
backplane, orthogonal PET substrates, and Ohmic Sheet surface-resistance films was established. 
By combining CST–Python co-simulation with genetic algorithm optimization, staged topology 
optimizations from 6×6 to 12×12 were carried out for the resistive-film pattern topology, geometric 
parameters, and independent X/Y sheet resistances. A final broadband absorbing structure was 
obtained with a reflection loss below −10 dB in the range of 4.80–18.00 GHz, a continuous 
effective bandwidth of 13.20 GHz, and a coverage of 82.5%. Its electromagnetic field distributions 
and energy dissipation mechanisms were systematically analyzed. 
The main innovative contributions of this thesis are summarized as follows: 
A dual-orthogonal resistive-film broadband absorbing unit model based on the Ohmic Sheet 
surface-resistance characterization was established. By introducing independent sheet resistances 
and differentiated pixel topologies in the X- and Y-directions, a functional division configuration of 
“one main dissipation layer + one impedance-adjustment/high-frequency-compensation layer” was 
proposed, overcoming the degrees-of-freedom limitation of conventional uniform resistive films 
and single-layer patterns in broadband absorption. 
An automated closed-loop optimization workflow integrating CST–Python co-simulation with 
a genetic algorithm was constructed, enabling hybrid encoding and global search of discrete pixel 
topology variables together with continuous geometric and sheet-resistance variables. A “semi-
constrained multi-template” strategy was proposed to balance search efficiency and structural 
interpretability in the high-resolution 12×12 topology. 
Through the staged optimizations of 6×6 → 9×9 → 12×12, the progressive influence of 
pixel resolution, independent layer sheet resistances, and pattern degrees of freedom on the 
broadband absorption performance was systematically revealed, and a final structure with a 
continuous effective bandwidth of 13.20 GHz was obtained. 
By combining the distributions of the electric field, magnetic field, and surface power loss 
density, it was elucidated that the broadband absorption arises from the synergistic mechanism of 
transmission suppression by the metal backplane, spatial coupling of the X/Y orthogonal sheet-
resistance films, joining of multiple local modes, and surface ohmic losses. 
Further in-depth research should be carried out in the following aspects: 
While keeping the structural thickness controllable, further optimize the low-frequency 
absorption boundary so that the effective absorption band extends below 2 GHz, covering a wider 
low-frequency microwave range. 
Investigate the absorption stability and bandwidth retention characteristics of the structure 
under different polarization modes and oblique incident angles, evaluating its engineering 
applicability in complex incident environments. 
Introduce actual fabrication parameters such as sheet-resistance deviation, substrate thickness 
tolerance, and assembly errors to analyze the performance robustness of the final structure, 
providing a basis for fabrication and testing.

Employ multi-objective optimization methods to seek a more balanced design among 
broadband absorption, lightweight and thin profile, manufacturability, and parameter robustness.
