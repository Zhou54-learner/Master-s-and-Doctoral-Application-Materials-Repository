# Variant A｜WAVE / OPT / MAT

## Draft

My undergraduate training in applied physics has gradually led me to a research question that I want to pursue more seriously in graduate study: how can structured physical systems be designed and understood so that their wave response is controlled in a predictable and physically interpretable way? My experience so far is still limited in scope, but it has given me a concrete entry point into this question through electromagnetic simulation, metamaterial structures and data-driven parameter exploration.

I completed my B.S. in Applied Physics at Harbin Institute of Technology in June 2026. My undergraduate thesis, supervised by Prof. Jun Li, focused on the simulation-guided design of resistive-film metamaterial structures for broadband microwave low reflection. Starting from a group-provided structural concept and reference code, I used and extended a Python–CST workflow for parameterized electromagnetic simulations and staged structural searches. I worked on 6×6, 9×9 and 12×12 pixel-pattern searches, expanded geometric, pattern and sheet-resistance variables, and adjusted candidate filtering and result processing. I also compared finite-thickness and Ohmic Sheet representations and examined how modeling choices affected the simulated reflection response. This project taught me that computational design is not only an optimization problem: the physical model, the parameterization and the evaluation criterion must remain mutually consistent if the result is to be scientifically meaningful.

The most important outcome of this work was therefore not a single optimized structure, but a research habit. I learned to connect a structural change to a physical response, to organize repeated simulations into a reproducible workflow, and to distinguish numerical evidence from stronger claims that would require fabrication, measurement or additional convergence studies. The archived design showed a co-polar reflection interval below -10 dB from 4.80 to 18.00 GHz within the 2–18 GHz scan window, but I treat this as a simulation result rather than a measured absorption performance. This distinction between what the model supports and what remains to be validated is something I want to preserve in future research.

My other undergraduate work broadened this perspective. In a national undergraduate innovation project on magnetic nanoalloy–graphene microwave-absorbing materials, I contributed to data processing, visualization, selected figures and manuscript preparation. I also participated in numerical data analysis for a two-dimensional dusty-plasma study, where I used MATLAB to process lattice-wave simulation data, analyze polarization characteristics and visualize particle trajectories. These experiences reinforced my interest in using quantitative modeling and data analysis to understand physical systems, even when the specific platform changes.

For graduate study, I do not want to simply repeat my undergraduate microwave project at another frequency scale. I am more interested in transferring the underlying training to a new physical platform: [plasmonics / nanophotonics / acoustic and mechanical metamaterials / integrated photonics / light–matter interaction]. The common thread for me is the relation between structure, material response and wave propagation. At the same time, I am aware of the limits of my present background. I do not yet have established experience in nanofabrication, optical or ultrafast spectroscopy, acoustic experimentation, FDTD, first-principles calculations or machine-learning model development. I see these not as credentials to claim, but as skills and physical methods that I would need to learn systematically in the right research environment.

I am particularly interested in [PI / project] because [one specific research reason based on the PI’s current work]. The project would allow me to contribute the simulation discipline and artificial-structure intuition I already have while moving into [target platform/problem]. I would like to begin from problems where numerical modeling and physical design remain closely connected, and then expand toward the experimental or advanced computational techniques required by the group.

My longer-term goal is to continue toward doctoral research. For me, an MPhil is not simply an additional degree but a period in which I hope to become a more independent researcher: capable of formulating a tractable question, choosing an appropriate model, validating the result against its assumptions, and communicating the work clearly. I am therefore looking for a programme in which I can work closely with a supervisor, receive rigorous research training and build a stronger foundation for later PhD study. I believe [programme / group] would offer an appropriate environment for this next stage.

## Draft

My interest in graduate research has increasingly shifted toward a broad physical question: how do interactions and local dynamics in many-body or complex systems develop into collective behaviour that can be described, measured and modeled at larger scales? I first encountered this question indirectly through undergraduate data analysis rather than through a dedicated soft-matter or active-matter project, and this is precisely why I want to pursue it more systematically in an MPhil programme.

I completed my B.S. in Applied Physics at Harbin Institute of Technology in June 2026. One part of my undergraduate research involved numerical data analysis for lattice waves in a two-dimensional dusty-plasma system. I used MATLAB to process simulation data, analyze polarization characteristics of particle motion, visualize trajectories and prepare selected figures used in the resulting publication. I do not regard this experience as prior work in active matter, living matter, colloids or complex fluids. What it gave me instead was an early quantitative view of an interacting many-particle system: local particle motion could be analysed in relation to collective wave behaviour, and careful data processing was necessary before a physical interpretation could be made.

This experience made me increasingly interested in collective dynamics and nonequilibrium phenomena. I would now like to move from a relatively narrow data-analysis role toward deeper research questions involving [soft condensed matter / active and living matter / colloidal crystallization / complex fluids / liquid crystals]. I am particularly attracted to systems in which microscopic or mesoscopic interactions produce emergent structures, phase behaviour or transport phenomena that are not obvious from the behaviour of an isolated constituent.

My undergraduate thesis provided a different but complementary form of training. Supervised by Prof. Jun Li, I worked on simulation-guided design of resistive-film metamaterial structures for broadband microwave low reflection. Starting from a group-provided structural concept and reference code, I used and extended a Python–CST workflow for parameterized electromagnetic simulations and staged structural searches. I expanded geometric and material variables, adjusted filtering and result processing, and compared alternative electromagnetic representations. Although this topic is not soft matter, it trained me to work with a structured computational workflow, examine the relationship between model assumptions and numerical output, and avoid treating an optimization result as more physically complete than the model allows.

Together, these projects shaped the way I would like to approach graduate research. I am comfortable learning through quantitative work, but I do not want to restrict myself to purely computational projects. For [target PI/group], I am interested in using modeling, simulation or quantitative image/data analysis as an entry point while developing the experimental and theoretical tools required by the system. At present I do not claim established expertise in CFD, molecular dynamics, machine learning, biological experiments, colloidal experiments or continuum modeling. These are areas I hope to learn through supervised research rather than skills I already possess.

I am especially interested in [PI / project] because [specific reason drawn from the PI’s present work]. The group’s work on [current research theme] provides a natural next step from my previous exposure to particle-level dynamics and quantitative analysis. I would like to contribute my existing experience in data processing and simulation workflows while learning how the group frames the underlying physics, designs experiments or models, and validates competing interpretations.

My long-term goal is doctoral research. I therefore see the MPhil as a period of transition from participating in defined undergraduate tasks to becoming capable of carrying a research problem with increasing independence. I want to strengthen my foundations in statistical and nonequilibrium physics, learn the relevant experimental/computational methods, and develop the ability to move from raw data or simulation output to a defensible physical argument. I am looking for a research environment where this development is possible through close supervision, rigorous discussion and sustained work on a well-defined problem.
