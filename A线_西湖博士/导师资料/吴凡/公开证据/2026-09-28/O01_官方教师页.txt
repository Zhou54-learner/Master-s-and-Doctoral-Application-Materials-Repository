The central theme of our research is to explore how physical systems can compute by harnessing their own natural dynamics. As artificial intelligence continues to grow in scale and complexity, conventional digital hardware faces increasing challenges in energy consumption, speed, and data movement. We ask whether light, waves, nonlinear dynamics, thermodynamic fluctuations, and programmable physical systems can provide a fundamentally different route toward future computing hardware.
One major direction of our research is optical thermodynamics. In highly multimode nonlinear optical systems, the enormous number of interacting optical modes makes a microscopic description extremely difficult. Instead of tracking every mode-to-mode interaction, we use concepts from statistical physics and thermodynamics, such as entropy, temperature, chemical potential, and thermal equilibrium, to describe the collective behavior of complex optical fields. This macroscopic viewpoint provides a powerful way to understand phenomena such as beam self-cleaning, optical thermalization, negative optical temperature, and the thermalization of optical orbital angular momentum. More importantly, it suggests a new computing principle: the random fluctuations of a thermalized optical field are not merely noise, but can encode useful information about the underlying physical system. By measuring optical correlations, we aim to develop optical thermodynamic computers capable of performing operations such as matrix inversion and solving linear systems, which are central to scientific computing and next-generation AI algorithms.
A second major direction is programmable integrated photonics and optical computing. Traditional inverse-designed photonic devices are usually fixed after fabrication, which limits their flexibility and makes them sensitive to fabrication imperfections. We develop reconfigurable integrated photonic platforms in which an external control light writes continuously tunable refractive-index landscapes into a chip. In this way, a photonic chip can be reprogrammed like software for different tasks. Combined with machine-learning-based feedback optimization, such systems can be trained directly in hardware to perform functions such as optical permutation, mode multiplexing, and matrix–vector multiplication. This approach opens a path toward trainable photonic chips, physical neural networks, and adaptive optical computing systems that can learn from their own measured outputs.
Looking forward, our group will pursue Optics–AI Co-design, a closed-loop research paradigm in which optics and artificial intelligence reinforce each other. On one side, optical physical systems can provide new computing primitives beyond conventional matrix–vector multiplication, including matrix inversion, physical optimization, and dynamics-based computation. On the other side, AI can be used to design, calibrate, and train complex photonic systems that are otherwise too difficult to control. By bringing together optical thermodynamics, programmable photonic chips, analog circuits, nonlinear dynamics, and machine learning, we aim to build a new class of intelligent hardware that computes not by imitating digital processors, but by exploiting what physical systems naturally do best.

In the Wu Lab, we conduct cutting-edge research at the intersection of photonics, artificial intelligence, statistical physics, nonlinear dynamics, analog computing, and unconventional computing. The group will focus on programmable integrated photonic chips, optical thermodynamic computing, physical neural networks, and next-generation physical computing hardware for artificial intelligence.
We welcome outstanding young researchers with backgrounds in photonics, physics, electrical engineering, integrated circuits, artificial intelligence, nanofabrication, nonlinear dynamics, or related interdisciplinary fields. Together, we aim to explore a new generation of machines that compute by harnessing the natural dynamics of physical systems.
We are currently recruiting postdoctoral researchers, with a particular interest in candidates with backgrounds in the following areas:
1.  Optical nanofabrication: candidates with experience in integrated photonic device design and fabrication, photonic chip testing, silicon photonics, lithium niobate photonics, thin-film photonic platforms, or related areas.
2.  Analog integrated circuit design: candidates with experience in analog or mixed-signal circuit design, on-chip driving and readout circuits, optoelectronic co-design, low-noise amplification, circuit modeling, or layout design.
